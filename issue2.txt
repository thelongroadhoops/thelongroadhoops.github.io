The Long Road - Issue 2

What is a "full ride"?

In case you are lucky (or talented) enough to earn a scholarship offer from a D1 school, there are still likely many questions about what that actually means.

A full scholarship covers the main costs of studying and living at the school: tuition, required fees and books, housing and meals. The package can also include money for other expenses, up to the school's official "cost of attendance" budget.

In our experience, many basketball players, especially upperclassmen, choose to live in off-campus apartments instead of traditional dorm rooms. For that purpose, schools can pay the housing and meals part of the scholarship as a cash stipend, based on the school's allowances rather than the player's actual rent and grocery bill.

That can leave money over if you spend less than the allowance. We have seen players use the difference as pocket money, rather than having to return it or having their stipend lowered. Every player that I know has also gotten their flight tickets covered by the school.

There are no set-in-stone rules on what is covered and what is not, so always remember to read the fine print and ask all the stupid questions you can think of!

Name, Image and Likeness - NOT Pay for Play?

Back in the day (= before 2021) the scholarship package was officially what schools could offer players. And we don't need to get into how some recruits' grandmas suddenly were able to move to a nicer house. Since then the landscape has been completely flipped around by the 3-letter-monster called NIL.

Name, Image & Likeness deals allow players to earn money from things like sponsorships and endorsements. Think equipment from a tennis sponsor or the local auto dealer giving the QB a new truck in exchange for smiling on a billboard. However, it didn't take long before the boosters figured out that funneling NIL-money to (mostly) football and basketball players would give a school a tremendous edge in attracting the best recruits. These booster groups became known as 'collectives' and the amounts of money they were able to put together completely changed the game.

Today the House settlement caps what schools can pay players at about 21,3M dollars per school for this season, up from 20,5M last season - and that is only the money the school is allowed to pay directly to their athletes. Star players are making millions of dollars and with that kind of money on the table only first round draft picks will routinely leave college early.

Based on conversations with schools over the past year, my picture of the market is that even at the mid-major levels players can make mid six-figure amounts comfortably. Still, most schools don't pay those six-figure amounts and the majority of incoming freshmen still "only" get a scholarship or at most a little pocket money on the side.

To me, the system now looks much like any other professional sport league where the biggest teams have the biggest budgets and the rookie players make (mostly) peanuts compared to their senior counterparts.

How the Nordic D1 players became D1 players

Words are good, numbers are better. These numbers show that almost half of the guys that came before took a layover at a different US level before landing at the bright lights of D1. Since the transfer portal has become such a big part of the game and schools are looking increasingly for the older, ready-to-play guys, the JuCo path appears to be the growing trend, whilst the top level prep schools are still the clearest US path into D1.

Closing: Food for thought

Since most talented young players in the whole world now strive to play D1, why would those D1 coaches take a look at you? In the first issue we covered that fewer than 1 in 100 US high school seniors makes a D1 roster as a freshman - now add the rest of the world and finding a way to stand out might start sounding like Mission Impossible. If you don't possess any of the obvious head turning qualities such as a 7foot frame, gorilla-like wingspan or a trampoline-like vertical jump it might be tough to get noticed. The numbers above show that of the 23 Nordic newcomers to D1 this season, 21 have played at a youth European Championship - a strong reminder of how valuable that stage can be for getting noticed. And that can also mean earning a scholarship to a Prep School (or JuCo) before being able to take that elusive jump to D1. There are a million B+ students - 6'2" shooting guards in basketball terms - so find a way to become A+ in at least one meaningful category. Shooting 50%+ from 3 will get noticed, averaging 6 assists to 1 turnover will get noticed and being a true vocal leader (= national team captain etc.) will get noticed. Your mother always said you are special - find a way to prove her right!
